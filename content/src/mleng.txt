@skill ml.stats_basics | Probability and statistics basics | - | 2 | 4
notes: Machine learning rests on probability. A probability lies between 0 and 1 and the probabilities of all outcomes sum to 1. Conditional probability P(A given B) is the chance of A when B is known to have happened, and Bayes' rule links it to the reverse: P(A given B) equals P(B given A) times P(A) divided by P(B). Two events are independent when P(A and B) equals P(A) times P(B). The expected value is the probability-weighted average, and variance measures spread around the mean; the standard deviation is its square root. A normal distribution is described by its mean and standard deviation, with about 68 percent of values within one standard deviation. The law of large numbers says sample averages approach the true mean. A small sample can mislead, so report uncertainty rather than a single number.

@skill ml.linear_algebra | Linear algebra for ML | - | 2 | 4
notes: Data and parameters in machine learning are vectors and matrices. A vector is a list of numbers and the dot product of two vectors multiplies matching entries and sums them, which measures alignment and is zero when they are perpendicular. A matrix times a vector applies a linear transformation, and multiplying an m by n matrix by an n by p matrix gives an m by p matrix; the inner dimensions must match and matrix multiplication is not commutative. The transpose flips rows and columns. The norm measures length, and the L2 norm is the square root of the sum of squares. Cosine similarity is the dot product divided by the product of norms. Batched operations with matrices make neural networks efficient, because one matrix multiplication computes all examples and neurons at once. Shapes are the most common source of bugs, so check them first.

@skill ml.train_test | Train, validation and test splits | ml.data_eda | 1 | 3
notes: To estimate how well a model will perform on new data you must evaluate it on examples it never trained on. Split the data into a training set to fit parameters, a validation set to choose hyperparameters and compare models, and a test set used once at the very end for the final unbiased estimate. A common split is 70, 15, 15 or 80, 10, 10 percent. Data leakage happens when information from outside the training set sneaks in, for example scaling with statistics computed on all data before splitting, or having the same patient or duplicate rows in both train and test. For time series, split by time so you never train on the future. For classification, stratify so class proportions are similar in every split. Repeatedly tuning on the test set quietly overfits to it, so it stops being a fair estimate.

@skill ml.metrics_cls | Classification metrics | ml.train_test,ml.stats_basics | 2 | 4
notes: Accuracy is the share of correct predictions but is misleading with imbalanced classes: a model that always predicts the majority class can score 99 percent while being useless. The confusion matrix counts true positives, false positives, true negatives and false negatives. Precision is true positives divided by predicted positives, answering how many flagged items were right. Recall is true positives divided by actual positives, answering how many real positives were found. F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. Raising the decision threshold usually increases precision and lowers recall. The ROC curve plots true positive rate against false positive rate across thresholds and AUC summarises it, where 0.5 is random guessing and 1.0 is perfect. Choose the metric from the cost of mistakes, such as high recall for disease screening.

@skill ml.metrics_reg | Regression metrics | ml.train_test,ml.stats_basics | 2 | 3
notes: Regression predicts a continuous number, so we measure the size of the error. Mean absolute error averages the absolute differences and is in the target's units and robust to outliers. Mean squared error averages the squared differences, penalising large errors heavily, and its square root RMSE returns to the target's units. R squared compares the model with always predicting the mean: 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than the mean, and it can be negative on new data for a bad model. A residual is the actual value minus the prediction, and a residual plot with visible patterns signals a missing relationship. Compare errors against a naive baseline such as predicting the average. Choose MAE when all errors matter equally and MSE or RMSE when large mistakes are especially costly.

@skill ml.linear_reg | Linear regression | ml.linear_algebra,ml.metrics_reg | 2 | 4
notes: Linear regression models the target as a weighted sum of features plus a bias: y equals w times x plus b. Training chooses weights that minimise the mean squared error between predictions and targets. The closed-form solution uses the normal equation, while gradient descent finds it iteratively and scales to large data. Each weight shows how much the prediction changes per unit increase of its feature with the others held fixed, which makes the model interpretable. The model assumes a linear relationship; if the relationship is curved you can add polynomial features. It is sensitive to outliers because errors are squared, and to correlated features, which make weights unstable. Evaluate on held-out data and inspect residuals. With many features relative to examples it overfits, which regularisation addresses. Linear regression is the baseline against which more complex models should be compared.

@skill ml.gradient_descent | Gradient descent | ml.linear_algebra,ml.linear_reg | 3 | 5
notes: Gradient descent minimises a loss function by repeatedly moving the parameters a small step in the direction opposite to the gradient, the direction of steepest increase: w becomes w minus the learning rate times the gradient. The learning rate controls the step size. Too large and the loss oscillates or diverges; too small and training is painfully slow. Batch gradient descent uses all examples per step, stochastic gradient descent uses one, and mini-batch uses a small group, which balances noise and speed and is the standard for deep learning. For convex losses such as linear regression the minimum is global; for neural networks the loss surface has many saddle points and local minima. Feature scaling makes the loss surface rounder and converges faster. Monitor the training loss curve, and stop or reduce the learning rate when it plateaus or becomes unstable.

@skill ml.logistic | Logistic regression | ml.linear_reg,ml.gradient_descent,ml.metrics_cls | 3 | 4
notes: Logistic regression is a linear classifier. It computes a weighted sum of the features and passes it through the sigmoid function, which squashes any real number into the range 0 to 1 and is interpreted as the probability of the positive class. A threshold, commonly 0.5, converts the probability to a class label and can be moved to trade precision against recall. The decision boundary is linear in the features. Training minimises the cross-entropy, or log loss, which punishes confident wrong predictions very strongly, using gradient descent. The model is called regression for historical reasons but it solves classification. For more than two classes use softmax, which turns scores into probabilities that sum to one. Weights can be read as changes in log-odds. Regularisation is usually added, and features should be scaled for stable training.

@skill ml.regularization | Regularization (L1 and L2) | ml.linear_reg,ml.gradient_descent | 3 | 3
notes: Regularisation reduces overfitting by adding a penalty on the size of the weights to the loss, discouraging the model from fitting noise. L2 regularisation, used in ridge regression, adds the sum of squared weights, shrinking all weights smoothly toward zero but rarely making them exactly zero. L1 regularisation, used in lasso, adds the sum of absolute weights and tends to drive some weights exactly to zero, which performs automatic feature selection. The strength is a hyperparameter, often called lambda or alpha: larger values mean simpler models that risk underfitting, and a value of zero means no regularisation. Choose it with cross-validation. Standardise features first, otherwise the penalty treats features unfairly. Other forms include dropout and early stopping in neural networks, and data augmentation. Regularisation trades a little bias for a larger reduction in variance.

@skill ml.bias_variance | Bias, variance and overfitting | ml.train_test,ml.regularization | 3 | 4
notes: Prediction error splits into bias, variance and irreducible noise. High bias means the model is too simple to capture the pattern, so it underfits: both training and validation errors are high. High variance means the model is too flexible and fits noise in the training data, so it overfits: training error is low but validation error is much higher. Learning curves that plot errors against training size or complexity show which problem you have. To reduce bias use a richer model or better features; to reduce variance get more data, regularise, simplify the model or use ensembling. Adding more data helps variance problems much more than bias problems. A large gap between training and validation scores signals overfitting. Noise in the labels sets a floor that no model can beat. Aim for the sweet spot, not the lowest training error.

@skill ml.cross_validation | Cross-validation and hyperparameter tuning | ml.bias_variance | 3 | 4
notes: Cross-validation estimates generalisation more reliably than a single split. In k-fold cross-validation the training data is divided into k folds; each fold takes a turn as the validation set while the model trains on the other k minus one folds, and the k scores are averaged. This costs k times as many trainings but uses data efficiently and shows variability across folds. Stratified folds keep class proportions, and grouped or time-aware folds avoid leakage across related rows. Hyperparameters, such as regularisation strength, tree depth or learning rate, are settings chosen before training, unlike parameters, which are learned. Grid search tries every combination, random search samples combinations and is often more efficient, and Bayesian methods guide the search. Tune with cross-validation on training data, then evaluate once on the untouched test set. Nested cross-validation gives an unbiased estimate when tuning and evaluating together.

@skill ml.knn | k-nearest neighbours and distance | ml.preprocessing,ml.linear_algebra | 2 | 3
notes: The k-nearest neighbours algorithm classifies a new point by finding the k closest training points under a distance measure and taking a majority vote, or averages their targets for regression. There is no training phase beyond storing the data, so it is called lazy, but prediction is slow on large datasets because it computes distances to every stored point unless an index structure is used. The most common distance is Euclidean, with Manhattan and cosine as alternatives. Because distances add up differences across features, features must be scaled, otherwise a feature with a large range dominates. A small k gives noisy, flexible boundaries that overfit, while a large k smooths too much and underfits; choose k by cross-validation and prefer an odd value for binary voting. In high dimensions distances become less meaningful, known as the curse of dimensionality.

@skill ml.trees | Decision trees | ml.metrics_cls,ml.bias_variance | 3 | 3
notes: A decision tree predicts by asking a sequence of yes-no questions about features, splitting the data into purer and purer groups until it reaches leaves that give a prediction. At each node the algorithm picks the split that most reduces impurity, measured for classification by Gini impurity or entropy and for regression by variance. Trees need no feature scaling, handle mixed feature types and are easy to interpret as rules. An unrestricted tree keeps splitting until it memorises the training data, so it overfits badly with high variance. Control complexity with maximum depth, minimum samples per leaf and pruning. Small changes in the data can produce a very different tree, so single trees are unstable. Trees split on axis-parallel thresholds, so they approximate diagonal boundaries with staircases. They are the building block of random forests and boosting.

@skill ml.quantization | Quantization and model compression | ml.nn_basics,ml.llm_basics | 4 | 4
notes: Quantization stores weights, and sometimes activations, with fewer bits than 32-bit floats, for example 8-bit or 4-bit integers, shrinking model size and memory traffic and often speeding up inference on hardware with fast integer arithmetic. A scale factor, and sometimes a zero point, maps integers back to real values. Weight-only quantization keeps activations in higher precision; quantizing both weights and activations, such as 4-bit weights with 8-bit activations, can be faster still.
